Thomas is right that MPL completeness requires valuations where `⊥` is not mapped to the algebra's bottom — that's exactly what `bot_val` (and `botForces` on the Kripke side) are for. The question is whether this freedom should come from making `⊥` an atom or from an explicit parameter on the evaluator.

The strongest reason to keep `⊥` as a primitive constructor that I can see is substitution invariance. Formulas over `Atom` form the free algebra over the signature `{⊥, →, ∧, ∨}`. Substitution `σ : Atom → Proposition Atom'` is the unique homomorphism extending `σ` — monadic `bind`. With primitive `⊥`, we get `| .bot => .bot` in the substitution function: `⊥` is a nullary operation in the signature, fixed by every substitution. Axiom schemes like `⊥ → A` are automatically closed under substitution — applying `σ` gives `⊥ → σ(A)`, not `σ(⊥) → σ(A)`.

With `⊥` as an atom, `bind σ` sends `⊥ ↦ σ(⊥)`, which can be anything. Every theorem about substitution closure (we currently have `subst_preserves_axiom`, `subst_preserves_intAxiom`, `subst_preserves_minAxiom`, `hilbertSubstitution`, `Theory.Derivation.substAtom`) acquires a side condition `σ(⊥) = ⊥`. The free monad on `Atom` is no longer free — you're working in a subcategory of `⊥`-preserving maps.

The same point manifests in universal algebra where `⊥` is a nullary operation symbol — same ontological kind as `→` and `∧`. Whereas atoms are generators, connectives (including nullary ones) are operations that determines what "homomorphism" means in that signature. With respect to logic, `⊥` along with the other connectives are the logical constants that contribute expressive power to the language with fixed semantic clauses, and the atoms provide the non-logical vocabulary that are subject to interpretation. That `⊥` deserves to be among the logical constants follows from the inadequacy of the `v ⊥ = ⊥` constraint which is external to the definition of satisfaction: it must be stated as a hypothesis, verified when constructing models, and preserved under model-theoretic operations (submodels, products, ultraproducts, canonical models).

These matters are obscured in minimal logic which has no axioms constraining the interpretation of `⊥`. Nevertheless, "arbitrary nullary operator" and "arbitrary atom" are not the same. An operator is fixed under substitution; an atom is not. Even though `⊥` is a logical constant, its interpretation in a given algebra is still a free parameter, and so needs to map it to something. The `bot_val` parameter provides exactly this: `⊥` evaluates to a fixed-but-unconstrained element `bot_val` of the algebra, the same element throughout the evaluation. With `⊥`-as-atom, substitution can replace `⊥` with a different formula entirely, which is a different degree of freedom than varying its interpretation.

Re cost comparison — the `bot_val` parameter adds one argument to `AlgEvaluate` and one field (`botForces`) to the Kripke semantics. With `⊥`-as-atom, every substitution theorem gets a side condition, every algorithm that builds valuations needs to ensure `v ⊥ = ⊥`, and `[Bot Atom]` propagates through every theorem statement. Matthew's DPLL point illustrates this pattern where the constraint `v ⊥ = ⊥` is doing the work of a semantic clause that should be part of the definition of satisfaction. Thomas's completeness theorem is genuinely elegant with the `v ⊨ T` framing — and it works just as well with primitive `⊥` and `bot_val`: the general theorem quantifies over `(v, bot_val)`, the `v ⊨ T` hypothesis does all the work, and specializing to IPL forces `bot_val = ⊥` via the efq axioms (since `bot_val ⇨ x = ⊤` for all `x` implies `bot_val ≤ x` for all `x`).

Anyhow, this is what I have come to so far, but curious to get your thoughts.

## PR Comment

**On bot as primitive vs atom:** The key distinction is that in minimal logic bot is an unconstrained *constant*, not an atom — no axioms constrain its denotation, but it's fixed under substitution. With primitive `⊥`, `subst` has `| .bot => .bot` and axiom scheme closure is automatic. With bot-as-atom, every substitution theorem acquires a side condition `σ(⊥) = ⊥` and the free monad on `Atom` is no longer free. The `bot_val` parameter gives interpretive freedom without substitutability; bot-as-atom gives both, which is more than the mathematics calls for. Practically, `[Bot Atom]` constraints also accumulate through every signature in the strong completeness work for propositional, modal, and bimodal logics, making the API noticeably heavier. The trade-off is an extra constructor case in recursions and the `bot_val` parameter in algebraic semantics, but I think these are the right costs. ## Explanation: "free monad" claim

With primitive `⊥`, `Proposition` is the free algebra over the signature `{⊥, →, ∧, ∨}` generated by `Atom`. Substitution (`bind`) is the free extension: every function `f : Atom → Proposition Atom'` extends to a unique homomorphism. No constraints on `f` — that's the universal property.

With `⊥`-as-atom, `Proposition` is still a free algebra — but over the signature `{→, ∧, ∨}` generated by `Atom` (which now includes `⊥` as a generator). The monad is still free. Every function `f : Atom → Proposition Atom'` extends uniquely, including functions that send `⊥` to arbitrary formulas. Nothing is broken at the type level.

The problem: by making `⊥` an atom, you've moved it from the operations to the generators. The free algebra you get is free over the wrong signature. The substitutions it generates are too permissive — they include ones that move `⊥`. To recover the intended behavior (axiom scheme closure, etc.), you restrict to `⊥`-preserving maps (`f(⊥) = ⊥`). In that subcategory the universal property fails: not every map on generators extends to a `⊥`-preserving homomorphism.

So the monad is free; the restriction to meaningful substitutions is not. Saying "the free monad is no longer free" is imprecise — the Zulip post says it but the PR comment uses the more careful "the intended substitutions no longer correspond to the full Kleisli category."
