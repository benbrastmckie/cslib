Thomas is right that MPL completeness requires valuations where `⊥` is not mapped to the algebra's bottom — that's exactly what `bot_val` (and `botForces` on the Kripke side) are for. The question is whether this freedom should come from making `⊥` an atom or from an explicit parameter on the evaluator.

The strongest reason to keep `⊥` as a primitive constructor that I can see is substitution invariance. Formulas over `Atom` form the free algebra over the signature `{⊥, →, ∧, ∨}`. Substitution `σ : Atom → Proposition Atom'` is the unique homomorphism extending `σ` — monadic `bind`. With primitive `⊥`, we get `| .bot => .bot` in the substitution function: `⊥` is a nullary operation in the signature, fixed by every substitution. Axiom schemes like `⊥ → A` are automatically closed under substitution — applying `σ` gives `⊥ → σ(A)`, not `σ(⊥) → σ(A)`.

With `⊥` as an atom, `bind σ` sends `⊥ ↦ σ(⊥)`, which can be anything. Every theorem about substitution closure (we currently have `subst_preserves_axiom`, `subst_preserves_intAxiom`, `subst_preserves_minAxiom`, `hilbertSubstitution`, `Theory.Derivation.substAtom`) acquires a side condition `σ(⊥) = ⊥`. The free monad on `Atom` is no longer free — you're working in a subcategory of `⊥`-preserving maps.

The same point manifests in universal algebra where `⊥` is a nullary operation symbol — same ontological kind as `→` and `∧`. Atoms are generators, connectives (including nullary ones) are operations. With respect to logic, `⊥` along with the other connectives are the logical constants that contribute expressive power to the language with fixed semantic clauses, and the atoms provide the non-logical vocabulary. The Johansson point is subtle: in minimal logic `⊥` has no axioms constraining its interpretation, but "arbitrary constant" ≠ "arbitrary variable." A constant is fixed under substitution; a variable is not. Johansson's `⊥` is a constant symbol in the signature with no non-logical axioms — not a variable. The `bot_val` parameter captures exactly this: `⊥` evaluates to a fixed-but-unconstrained element `bot_val` of the algebra, the same element throughout the evaluation. With `⊥`-as-atom, substitution can replace `⊥` with a different formula entirely, which is a different degree of freedom than varying its interpretation.

Re cost comparison — the `bot_val` parameter adds one argument to `AlgEvaluate` and one field (`botForces`) to the Kripke semantics. With `⊥`-as-atom, every substitution theorem gets a side condition, every algorithm that builds valuations (DPLL, as mentioned) needs to ensure `v ⊥ = ⊥`, and `[Bot Atom]` propagates through every theorem statement. Thomas's completeness theorem is genuinely elegant with the `v ⊨ T` framing — and it works just as well with primitive `⊥` and `bot_val`: the general theorem quantifies over `(v, bot_val)`, the `v ⊨ T` hypothesis does all the work, and specializing to IPL forces `bot_val = ⊥` via the efq axioms (since `bot_val ⇨ x = ⊤` for all `x` implies `bot_val ≤ x` for all `x`).

For what it's worth, every Lean 4 formalization I've found (FormalizedFormalLogic/Foundation, Trufaş 2024) uses primitive `⊥`, as do the standard proof-theory references (Troelstra-Schwichtenberg, van Dalen).
